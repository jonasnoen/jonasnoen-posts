Guide: Buy check before you click

Only buy what you really need on sale: Claude checks the need and the catches, gives a clear verdict (buy, wait, skip) and tells you what to verify yourself.

How it works

  1. Collect the facts. Copy the product name, model and key specs from the store page, plus the price, the "was" price and the store. Write down honestly what you need it for and what you already own.
  2. Run the buy check. Paste prompt 1 into a new chat and fill in the gaps. Claude asks up to 3 follow-up questions, then gives you need, fit, catches, verdict and a check-yourself list.
  3. Check it yourself. Work through the list: price history over the last weeks (compare with that, not with the crossed-out price – in the EU, stores announcing a discount must show their lowest price of the previous 30 days), the same item in 2–3 other stores, return policy, warranty, shipping.
  4. Torn between options? If you can't decide between two models (or not buying at all), use prompt 2 for a comparison table.
  5. Impulse brake. Verdict was "wait" but you still want to click? Prompt 3 asks you 5 honest questions – then you decide.

Prompt 1: The buy check → verdict + check-yourself list

You are my honest buying advisor. I'm thinking about buying something on sale. Help me make a clear decision – don't talk me into buying it.

PRODUCT (name, model, key specs – feel free to paste them from the product page):
___
PRICE NOW: ___ | "Was" price shown: ___ | Store: ___
WHAT I NEED IT FOR (how often, for what exactly): ___
WHAT I ALREADY HAVE (similar item, how old, does it still work?): ___
MY BUDGET: ___

Work like this:
1. NEED: Ask me up to 3 short questions if something important is missing. Otherwise check: does this product solve a real problem, or does it just replace something that still works?
2. DOES IT FIT? Compare the specs with my purpose: what matters, what am I paying for that I don't need, what's missing?
3. THE CATCHES: List possible red flags (e.g. unclear "was" price, countdown pressure, last year's model, shipping/accessories not included, unknown seller). Clearly mark what you're only guessing.
4. VERDICT: BUY, WAIT or SKIP – with 3 reasons, one sentence each.
5. CHECK YOURSELF: A checklist of what I need to look up myself before I click, because you may not know current prices and store terms.

Rules: Don't invent prices, test results or specs. If you don't know something, write "check yourself". Keep it short, in bullet points.

For every deal that tempts you – before you add it to the cart.

Prompt 2: Compare: A, B or nothing

I can't decide between these options:
A) ___ (price: ___)
B) ___ (price: ___)
C) buy nothing / keep using what I have

My purpose: ___
What matters most to me (in order): ___

Make a table with the columns: Option | Price | Fits my purpose (1–5) | What I pay for but don't need | Catches. Only use the data I give you and mark gaps with "missing". Below it: your recommendation in 2 sentences and the one question that would change my decision the most.

When you're torn between two products or thinking about keeping what you have.

Prompt 3: The impulse brake

Before I hit "Buy": ask me 5 honest questions, one at a time, that test whether I really need this or just want it because of the discount (e.g. "Would you buy it at full price?", "What will you do with it in 30 days?"). Wait for my answer each time. Then sum up in 3 sentences what my answers say about this purchase and give me a clear recommendation: buy, put it on my wishlist, or skip.

When the verdict was "wait" or "skip" and you still want to buy.

Example

Sam sees over-ear headphones for $179 "was $299", countdown running. He commutes 2 hours a day; his earbuds are a year old and still work. Prompt 1 says: fits the purpose (noise cancelling for commuting), but no urgent problem; catches: unclear whether $299 was ever the real price, the countdown creates pressure. Verdict: WAIT – check the price history and two other stores first. Sam finds it was $189 a few weeks ago, so the saving is much smaller than it looks – it goes on his wishlist.

Check before you send

  • Clear verdict: buy, wait or skip – with reasons
  • Checked the price history yourself (not just the crossed-out price)
  • Compared 2–3 other stores, return policy and warranty
  • Honestly answered: would you buy it at full price?

Before any sale purchase, paste this into a new chat, fill in the gaps (copy the specs from the product page) and wait for the verdict plus the check-yourself list.

Guide: Find and get rid of forgotten subscriptions – with Claude

In about 15 minutes, see every recurring payment in your bank statement, decide what goes, have the cancellation messages ready and get a subscription calendar for next year.

How it works

  1. Export your statement. In your online banking, download your transactions for the last 3–12 months as CSV or PDF. The longer the period, the more likely yearly charges show up too.
  2. Black out personal data. Remove or black out your name, account number, card number and address. With a CSV, it's enough to keep only the columns date, payee, description and amount.
  3. Let Claude find the subscriptions. Upload the file and send prompt 1. Claude builds the table with cost per month and per year and flags anything odd with ⚠️. The math is shown – double-check it quickly.
  4. Decide and cancel. Prompt 2 sorts everything into keep, cancel and check. Prompt 3 writes the cancellation messages. Many services also let you cancel directly in your online account or app settings – keep the confirmation.
  5. Set up a subscription calendar. Prompt 4 turns what's left into a calendar so yearly charges never surprise you again. Repeat the check once a year.

Prompt 1: Subscription detective: find every recurring payment

You are my subscription detective. I'm uploading my bank statement for the last ___ months (CSV or PDF – I've blacked out my name, account number and address).

Your task:
1. Find every recurring payment: subscriptions, memberships, insurance, contracts, app purchases, donations – including yearly and quarterly ones, and small amounts under $5.
2. Put them in a table: Payee | What it probably is | Billing cycle | Amount per charge | Cost per month | Cost per year | Last charged on.
3. Show how you calculated the yearly cost (e.g. 9.99 × 12) and give the total per month and per year below the table.
4. Flag with ⚠️ anything unusual: price increases, duplicate services (e.g. two streaming or two cloud storage plans), free trials that turned into paid plans, payees you can't identify for sure.
5. End with 3 questions for me that help me decide what can go.

Rules: Don't invent payments or amounts. If you're not sure about a payee, write [UNCLEAR] instead of guessing. One-off purchases (groceries, gas, online orders) don't belong in the table.

First – with your uploaded, blacked-out bank statement.

Prompt 2: Keep, cancel or check?

Here's my subscription table from the last step. Help me decide.

For each line, here's how often I actually use it (bullet points): ___

Sort everything into three groups:
- KEEP (I use it regularly, price is fine)
- CANCEL (barely used or a duplicate)
- CHECK (cheaper plan, yearly instead of monthly, family/sharing option – phrased as a question only, no made-up prices)

Below that, tell me how much I save per year if I cancel everything under CANCEL (show the math).

In the same chat, right after prompt 1.

Prompt 3: All cancellation messages in one go

Write a short cancellation message (email or letter) for every subscription under CANCEL.

My details: name ___, address ___, customer/member number per provider ___

Each message includes:
- a subject line with the word "Cancellation" and my customer number
- cancellation at the earliest possible date allowed by my contract
- a request to confirm the cancellation and the exact end date in writing
- a request not to send me any win-back offers
- polite, short, no reason given

If a detail is missing, write [ADD]. Below, add one sentence on what to watch out for when sending (e.g. use the cancel option in my online account if there is one, and keep the confirmation).

Once you know what should go.

Prompt 4: Subscription calendar for next year

Turn my KEEP list into a subscription calendar for next year.

Table: Month | What gets charged | Amount | Note.
Highlight yearly charges separately so they don't surprise me.
Below, give me my fixed monthly cost for subscriptions and suggest a day of the year to repeat this subscription check.

At the end, so nothing surprises you anymore.

Example

Example (made up): Sam uploads transactions from May to October. Claude finds five recurring payments – streaming $13.99, gym $29.90, cloud storage $2.99, music app $10.99 per month and a magazine for $59 per year – $753.44 per year in total. Sam had forgotten about the cloud storage and the magazine. Prompt 2 puts the gym and the magazine under CANCEL, prompt 3 writes both cancellations, and Sam adds the member numbers.

Check before you send

  • Name, account number and address blacked out before uploading?
  • Yearly costs double-checked and [UNCLEAR] lines assigned yourself?
  • Notice period checked in the contract or account, and confirmation kept?
  • Subscription calendar saved and next check in your calendar?

Export your bank statement as CSV or PDF, black out name/account number/address, upload it and send the prompt – Claude lists every recurring payment with its yearly cost.

Guide: Client quotes and invoices with Claude – no more Word fiddling

Turn a few bullet points into a clear quote with limits, stress-test it before sending, follow up cleanly and check the invoice before it goes out.

How it works

  1. Collect your bullet points. Client, job, your price or hourly rate, timeline, payment terms, currency – and whether you're registered for sales tax/VAT. That's all Prompt 1 needs.
  2. Build the quote. Send Prompt 1 and answer the follow-up questions. Pay attention to the “Not included” section – it protects you from unpaid extra work.
  3. Close the gaps. Prompt 2 in the same chat: Claude reads it like a difficult client, suggests tighter wording and outputs the revised quote.
  4. Follow up. No reply? Prompt 3 gives you two short emails for day 7 and day 14.
  5. Check the invoice. Before sending, run Prompt 4: a table of the essentials, the math and a match against the quote. Country-specific tax questions go to your tax authority or accountant.

Prompt 1: Quote from bullet points

You're my assistant for writing client quotes as a freelancer. Turn my bullet points into a finished, professional quote.

My bullet points:
- Me: ___ (name, what I do, registered for sales tax/VAT yes/no)
- Client: ___
- Job: ___
- My price or hourly rate: ___
- Timeline: ___
- Payment: ___
- Currency: ___

Structure:
1. A short intro that sums up the client's goal in one sentence.
2. Line-item table: No. | Item | Description (exactly what's included) | Quantity | Price. Use only my prices and never invent amounts – if something is missing, write [PRICE MISSING].
3. An "Assumptions" section (e.g. who provides content, how many rounds of revisions).
4. A "Not included" section with at least 3 things the client might otherwise silently expect.
5. Timeline, payment terms and how long the quote is valid.
6. Subtotal and total – add a tax/VAT line only if I said I'm registered, and mark the rate as [CHECK RATE] unless I gave it.

Tone: clear, friendly, no filler. After the quote, ask me up to 5 questions about anything that's still unclear.

First, for every request.

Prompt 2: Gap check: Claude as the difficult client

Read the quote above like a critical client who wants as much as possible for their money.
1. Which lines could they read as "more work included"? Quote them and give me a tighter version of each.
2. What's missing to make it clear when the work counts as "done" (sign-off)?
3. Which extra requests are likely – and how do I state what they cost (e.g. per page, per hour)?
4. Give me a list of questions I should ask the client before sending.
At the end, output the revised version of the quote.

Right after, in the same chat.

Prompt 3: Follow-up emails

On ___ I sent a quote for ___ to ___ and haven't heard back.
Write 2 short follow-up emails (max 80 words each):
1. friendly, after 7 days,
2. clear, after 14 days, mentioning that the quote is valid until ___.
No pressure, no apologizing. End each one with exactly one concrete question that's quick to answer.

If there's no reply after a week.

Prompt 4: Invoice check (essentials)

Check this invoice before I send it. I'm based in ___ and my client is based in ___. Go through the list one by one and output a table: Item | present? (yes/no/unclear) | where it is or what's missing.
1. My full name/business name and address
2. Client's full name and address
3. Invoice date
4. Unique, sequential invoice number
5. Description of the work and quantity
6. Date or period of service
7. Line amounts, subtotal and total, currency
8. Tax ID and tax/VAT rate + amount – only if I'm registered; otherwise check that no tax is charged by mistake
9. Payment terms and due date
10. How to pay me (bank details or payment link)
Also check: do all the numbers add up? Does the invoice match the quote (items, prices)?
Then tell me which invoice requirements for my country you're not sure about, so I can check them with my tax authority or accountant. Important: you don't replace tax advice. If this is a special case (cross-border, reverse charge, VAT exemption), say so and recommend getting it checked.
Invoice: ___
Quote (optional): ___

Before the invoice goes out.

Example

Web designer, client Northside Coffee (fictional), 5-page website for $3,300, January–February, 50% upfront. Claude turns it into three line items (concept & design $1,200, build $1,800, training $300) with “Not included: copywriting, photos, hosting”. The gap check asks: who delivers the copy and by when, what does each extra page cost, when is the website “done”? The invoice check flags a missing date of service and a missing due date.

Check before you send

  • Every price comes from you – no amounts estimated by Claude.
  • “Not included” and a clear sign-off (“done when …”) are in the quote.
  • The invoice has all the essentials from slide 7 and matches the quote.
  • Tax/VAT and cross-border questions checked with your tax authority or accountant.

Fill in your bullet points and send – Claude builds the quote, then asks you about anything that's still unclear.

Guide: Your personal Claude profile

Introduce yourself to Claude properly once: build a profile with an interview prompt, paste it into your settings and get better-fitting answers in every chat – without explaining yourself again each time.

How it works

  1. Start the interview. Paste Prompt 1 into a new chat. Claude asks 8 questions, one at a time. Keep your answers short – bullet points are fine.
  2. Get your profile text. At the end you get a finished first-person text (about 150 words max) with concrete rules – plus a list of what Claude deliberately left out.
  3. Paste it into your settings. Click your initials in the bottom left → Settings → the section for your personal instructions (depending on the version, e.g. "Your Claude" or "Account") → the instructions field. Paste, save. According to Claude's help center, it applies to all your chats.
  4. Test it. Open a new chat and send Prompt 2 with a typical task of yours. Do tone and length fit? If not: make that rule in your profile more concrete.
  5. Sharpen it after a week. Collect 2–3 answers that annoyed you and give them to Prompt 3 together with your profile. For single topics, set up a project with its own instructions instead – those only apply there.

Prompt 1: The interview → your profile text

You're helping me write a personal profile for Claude that I'll paste into my settings (personal instructions). It should apply in every chat.

Interview me with exactly 8 questions. Ask only ONE question at a time, wait for my answer, then ask the next:
1. What do you do for work, and what are you working on most right now?
2. What do you mainly want to use Claude for? (3 typical tasks)
3. How should Claude sound? (e.g. casual or formal, playful or straight to the point)
4. How long should answers usually be?
5. Which format do you like? (bullet points, table, prose, step by step)
6. What do you already know well – what does Claude NOT need to explain to you?
7. What annoys you in AI answers? (pet peeves)
8. What should Claude always do? (e.g. ask clarifying questions, push back honestly, flag anything uncertain)

If an answer is unclear, ask one short follow-up.

Then deliver:
A) MY PROFILE: a finished text in first person, 150 words max, organised under "About me", "What I use Claude for", "How to answer me", "Please never". Concrete rules instead of adjectives (instead of "be brief": "5 sentences max unless I ask for more").
B) 3 things you deliberately left out because they're too private or too specific for every chat.

Rules: Don't include sensitive data (home address, phone number, passwords, health details), even if I mention it. Don't add anything I didn't say.

Once at the start, in a new chat.

Prompt 2: The test in a new chat

Quick test: summarise in 3 bullet points what you know about me and how I want my answers, based on my personal instructions.

Then do this typical task of mine: ___

Stick to my instructions and, at the end, name in one line which of them you applied.

Right after saving the profile in your settings – in a NEW chat.

Prompt 3: Sharpen your profile

Here is my current Claude profile:
___

And here are 2–3 answers from you that I did NOT like, each with one sentence on why:
___

Revise my profile: figure out which rule was missing or too vague. Give me the new version (150 words max) and below it a short list: changed · new · removed. Keep everything that works.

After about a week, or whenever answers stop fitting again.

Example

Lena is a freelance photographer (weddings, business portraits). After the interview her profile says, among other things: "Answer me casually and briefly, 5 sentences max, bullet points preferred. Client emails: polite and warm, never cheesy. I know cameras and lighting – don't explain them to me. Please never: filler like 'Great question!', emojis, summaries at the end." In a new chat she sends Prompt 2 with "Write a reply to a wedding inquiry" and checks whether tone and length fit without further explanation.

Check before you send

  • Profile is about 150 words max – concrete rules instead of adjectives
  • No sensitive data (address, phone number, passwords, health) in the profile
  • Tested in a new chat with Prompt 2
  • Topic-specific things (one client, one project) go into project instructions, not your profile

Paste it into a new chat and answer the 8 questions briefly – copy the finished profile into Claude's settings under personal instructions; from then on it applies to all your chats.

Guide: Turn a note into a finished reel script

Use three prompts that build on each other to turn a few bullet points into a ready-to-record short-video script – with a strong opening, the right length and ideas for your next videos.

How it works

  1. Collect your note. Write 3–6 bullet points: the topic, the one message that should stick, an example from your own life. No full sentences needed.
  2. Get the script. Prompt 1 with your note and setup (platform, length, audience, tone, format). Claude returns 3 hooks, the script as a table, a CTA and a caption.
  3. Cut it and check the hook. Prompt 2 in the same chat. Claude counts the words, cuts to your target length and rates the hooks. Take the best one – or your own, if it sounds better.
  4. Read it out loud, then record. Read the script out loud once. Anything that feels awkward, rephrase in your own words. Look up everything marked [CHECK] before you record.
  5. Plan a series. Prompt 3 turns the topic into 5 more ideas. You can paste each one straight back into Prompt 1.

Prompt 1: The reel script from your note

You are my script writer for short videos (Reels/TikTok). Turn my note into a finished script I can record right away.

My note (bullet points are fine):
___

Setup:
- Platform: ___ (Instagram Reels / TikTok / both)
- Length: ___ seconds (default: 20)
- Audience: ___
- Tone: ___ (e.g. casual, bold, calm and explanatory)
- Format: ___ (I talk to camera / voiceover only / text on screen only)
- At the end, the viewer should: ___ (e.g. save, comment, follow)

Deliver:
1. HOOKS: 3 options for the first 2 seconds, 10 words max, as a statement, not a question. One line each: hook – why it works.
2. SCRIPT as a table: Second | Spoken (word for word) | Visual/action | Text overlay. Show the result or the payoff by second 2–3 at the latest, not at the end.
3. Assume about 2.5 spoken words per second. If the text doesn't fit the length, cut a point rather than rushing.
4. CTA: one sentence that matches what the viewer should do at the end.
5. CAPTION: 3 short paragraphs + 4–6 hashtags.

Rules: Don't invent numbers, studies or results that aren't in my note. If a claim needs facts you don't have, mark it with [CHECK]. If something important is missing, ask at most 2 questions first.

As soon as you have an idea – even if it's just three bullet points.

Prompt 2: Cut + hook check

Review the script above like a strict short-form video editor.

1. HOOK CHECK for all 3 hooks: Is it a statement, not a question? 10 words max? Does it spark curiosity without lying? Rate each hook 1–5 with a one-sentence reason, then rewrite the best one even sharper.
2. LENGTH: Count the spoken words and estimate the duration (about 2.5 words per second). If the script is longer than ___ seconds, cut it to that length: remove filler words, repetition and the weakest point – not the payoff.
3. FIRST 3 SECONDS: What exactly do you see and hear? Is there a reason not to swipe away? If not: a concrete suggestion to fix it.
4. Show the shortened script as a table again (Second | Spoken | Visual/action | Text overlay) and list below it what you cut.

In the same chat, right after Prompt 1.

Prompt 3: Turn one topic into a series

Turn the topic of my script into a short series.

1. Suggest 5 more reel ideas on the same topic, each from a different angle (e.g. mistakes, before/after, myth vs. truth, step by step, personal story).
2. For each idea: working title, hook (statement, 10 words max), 3 bullet points for the content.
3. Mark the 2 ideas that fit my audience ___ best and explain briefly why.
4. Write the bullet points so I can paste each idea straight back into Prompt 1.

Once the first script is done and you want to plan your next videos.

Example

Note: "Sunday meal prep, 3 common mistakes, my trick: just 2 dishes, goal: calmer weekdays". Setup: Instagram Reels, 20 seconds, busy professionals, casual, I talk to camera. Claude suggests hooks like "Stop cooking five dishes every Sunday", then the table: 0–2 s hook line, 2–8 s show the finished boxes in the fridge, 8–15 s the three mistakes with overlay 1 · 2 · 3, 15–20 s CTA. Prompt 2 notes that the text would take about 34 seconds, drops one mistake and cuts it to 20 seconds.

Check before you send

  • The hook is a statement, not a question, and has 10 words max.
  • The result or payoff comes in the first 2–3 seconds.
  • The spoken text fits the length (about 2.5 words per second).
  • No number or claim that doesn't come from your note – otherwise [CHECK].

Fill in your bullet points and the setup (platform, length, audience, tone) – Claude returns 3 hooks, the script as a table with seconds and overlays, a CTA and a caption.

Guide: Clean up your Downloads folder with Claude

Turn a chaotic file list into a finished cleanup plan with three prompts – folders, new names, a declutter list – and, if you want, carry it out with a script that does a dry run first.

How it works

  1. Get your file list. Mac: open Terminal and type ls ~/Downloads | pbcopy – the list is now on your clipboard. Windows: open PowerShell and type Get-ChildItem $HOME\Downloads -Name | Set-Clipboard. Only names – no file contents leave your computer.
  2. Get the plan. Prompt 1 with folder name, purpose and the list. Claude returns a tree, a naming scheme, an old → new table with confidence, and a "can probably go" list.
  3. Fine-tune. Answer the questions and adjust the table with Prompt 2. Quickly open and check every row rated "low" by hand.
  4. Carry it out. Move files by hand – or let Prompt 3 write a script. Make a backup first, look at the dry run, then run the real version.
  5. Stay tidy. Save the 5 rules from Prompt 2 as a note. Once a month, run your new files through Prompt 1 again.

Prompt 1: Cleanup plan

I want to clean up a folder. Below is the list of all file names (one name per line). You only see the names, not the contents – so don't guess what's inside a file; only infer from the name and extension.

Do the following:
1. FOLDER STRUCTURE: Suggest at most 6–8 main folders (with a number in front, e.g. 01_Invoices) that match what's actually in the list. Show them as a tree.
2. NAMING SCHEME: One consistent scheme following YYYY-MM_Topic_Version (e.g. 2026-03_Lease_v1.pdf). If no date is recognizable, leave YYYY-MM as a gap.
3. TABLE: | old name | new name | target folder | confidence (high/medium/low) |. For "low", say briefly why.
4. CAN PROBABLY GO: A list of likely duplicates (e.g. "File (1).pdf"), old installers (.dmg/.exe/.msi) and empty/untitled files – each with a reason. Don't mark anything as "delete" yourself, I decide.
5. QUESTIONS: At most 3 questions that would clearly improve the sorting.

My folder is called: ___
What I use it for: ___
File list:
___

First: turn the file list into folders, a naming scheme, a table and a declutter list.

Prompt 2: Fine-tune + rules

Thanks. Now adjust the table to my answers:
- My answers to your questions: ___
- These folders I want renamed / merged: ___
- These files stay where they are: ___

Then give me:
1. the updated table (old name | new name | target folder),
2. a short rule list for the future (max. 5 rules: where new downloads go and how I name them),
3. all rows with confidence "low" collected again, so I can check them by hand.

Right after, in the same chat, once you've answered the questions or want something different.

Prompt 3: Script with dry run

Write me a script from the final table that creates the folders and renames and moves the files.
- My system: ___ (Mac/Linux = shell script, Windows = PowerShell)
- Path to the folder: ___

Rules for the script:
1. It DELETES nothing. Files from "can probably go" only go into a folder 99_Review.
2. If a target name already exists, nothing is overwritten – append _2.
3. First a dry run that only shows what would happen (Mac/Linux: echo instead of mv, Windows: -WhatIf). Only when I write "go" do you give me the real version.
4. Explain in 3 sentences how I run the script, and that I should make a backup first.

Optional, if you don't want to move 200 files by hand. Dry run first, then the real version.

Example

Downloads folder with 140 files (example): Claude suggests 01_Invoices, 02_Contracts, 03_Photos, 04_Apps, 05_Other, turns scan0001.pdf into "2026-03_Lease_v1.pdf" (confidence medium – the name alone isn't clear) and collects setup_final2.dmg and Invoice(3).pdf under "can probably go". After Prompt 3, the dry run shows every planned move before anything is moved.

Check before you send

  • Checked every row rated "low" by hand?
  • Backup made before any script runs?
  • Looked at the dry run – nothing gets deleted or overwritten?
  • Saved the rules for new downloads?

Paste the list of file names (Mac: ls in Terminal, Windows: Get-ChildItem -Name in PowerShell) – Claude plans folders, new names and what can go; you do the moving.

Guide: Find the gift that actually fits with Claude

Go from “no idea” to a gift that fits, a before-you-buy check and a personal card message in one flow – for one person or your whole list.

How it works

  1. Fill in the gift profile. Who it's for, budget, what they love, what they already have, what's a hard no, one shared memory – the save slide helps.
  2. Get ideas. Send prompt 1 and answer Claude's questions. You get 10 ideas in 3 price tiers with reasons plus 2 experiences. Not quite right? Reply “less tech, more time together”.
  3. Compare your top 3. Prompt 2: a table with how much they'll love it, the “already has it” risk and what you must check before buying – plus a plan B.
  4. Write the card. Prompt 3 with a real memory: three versions, pick one (or mix them).
  5. Everyone at once. Prompt 4 when you have several gifts to buy: split the budget, order of purchases, emergency ideas.

Prompt 1: Gift advisor (ideas)

You're my gift advisor. First ask me these questions and wait for my answers before suggesting anything:
1. Who is the gift for (relationship, age)?
2. What's the budget?
3. What does this person love (hobbies, favorite topics, what they do on weekends)?
4. What do they already have, or get every year anyway?
5. What's a hard no (decor, alcohol, allergies, more clutter)?
6. Is there a shared memory or inside joke?

Then create:
- 10 gift ideas in 3 price tiers (below budget, within budget, slightly above) – each with one sentence "Why this fits", based on my answers.
- 2 experience ideas (time instead of things).
- No generic picks like scented candles, socks or gift cards unless I ask for them.
- Prices only as a rough range, with a note that you can't check them live.

My answers: ___

First, once per person.

Prompt 2: Compare top 3 + before-you-buy check

Shortlist these 3 ideas from your list: ___
Compare them in a table:
1. How much will the person probably love it (with reasons from my answers)?
2. Risk "already has it" or "doesn't fit" – and how I can find out without spoiling the surprise.
3. What I have to check myself before buying (delivery by ___, size, availability, price in the shop).
4. How to make the gift more personal (wrapping, a small add-on that costs very little).
End with a clear recommendation and a plan B in case my first choice doesn't arrive in time.
