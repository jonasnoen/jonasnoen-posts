Guide: Find the gift that actually fits with Claude

Go from “no idea” to a gift that fits, a before-you-buy check and a personal card message in one flow – for one person or your whole list.

How it works

  1. Fill in the gift profile. Who it's for, budget, what they love, what they already have, what's a hard no, one shared memory – the save slide helps.
  2. Get ideas. Send prompt 1 and answer Claude's questions. You get 10 ideas in 3 price tiers with reasons plus 2 experiences. Not quite right? Reply “less tech, more time together”.
  3. Compare your top 3. Prompt 2: a table with how much they'll love it, the “already has it” risk and what you must check before buying – plus a plan B.
  4. Write the card. Prompt 3 with a real memory: three versions, pick one (or mix them).
  5. Everyone at once. Prompt 4 when you have several gifts to buy: split the budget, order of purchases, emergency ideas.

Prompt 1: Gift advisor (ideas)

You're my gift advisor. First ask me these questions and wait for my answers before suggesting anything:
1. Who is the gift for (relationship, age)?
2. What's the budget?
3. What does this person love (hobbies, favorite topics, what they do on weekends)?
4. What do they already have, or get every year anyway?
5. What's a hard no (decor, alcohol, allergies, more clutter)?
6. Is there a shared memory or inside joke?

Then create:
- 10 gift ideas in 3 price tiers (below budget, within budget, slightly above) – each with one sentence "Why this fits", based on my answers.
- 2 experience ideas (time instead of things).
- No generic picks like scented candles, socks or gift cards unless I ask for them.
- Prices only as a rough range, with a note that you can't check them live.

My answers: ___

First, once per person.

Prompt 2: Compare top 3 + before-you-buy check

Shortlist these 3 ideas from your list: ___
Compare them in a table:
1. How much will the person probably love it (with reasons from my answers)?
2. Risk "already has it" or "doesn't fit" – and how I can find out without spoiling the surprise.
3. What I have to check myself before buying (delivery by ___, size, availability, price in the shop).
4. How to make the gift more personal (wrapping, a small add-on that costs very little).
End with a clear recommendation and a plan B in case my first choice doesn't arrive in time.

Right after the ideas.

Prompt 3: A card message that sounds like you

Write a card message for ___ to go with the gift ___.
Use this shared memory: ___
Give me 3 versions: warm, funny and short (max. 2 sentences).
Rules: it should sound like me, not like a store-bought greeting card – no clichés like "warmest wishes this holiday season" or "with all my love". If the memory doesn't fit the tone, tell me.

Once the gift is decided.

Prompt 4: Gift list for everyone

I need gifts for several people. Total budget: ___
People (each: name, relationship, 2–3 keywords): ___
Create:
1. A table: person – share of the budget – 2 ideas – "why" in one sentence.
2. Split the budget fairly, weighted by how close the relationship is, and show the total.
3. A shopping order: what to order first (long delivery times), what you can buy in a store.
4. For each person, one low-cost backup idea as an emergency plan.

When you have several gifts and one total budget.

Example

Mom, 62, loves her garden and crime novels, already has every kitchen gadget, doesn't want more decor, budget $30–60. Claude suggests, among others, heirloom vegetable seeds (below budget), a murder-mystery dinner game for the family (within budget) and a day together at a garden show (experience). In the comparison the mystery game wins, the seeds are plan B, and the card message jokes that this year the whole family is on the case.

Check before you send

  • Price, delivery time and availability checked in the shop yourself – Claude doesn't look them up live.
  • “Already has it” risk cleared without spoiling the surprise.
  • Plan B ready in case delivery is late.
  • Card message includes a real memory and sounds like you.

Send the prompt, answer Claude's 6 questions – then you get 10 ideas in 3 price tiers plus 2 experiences.

Guide: Your trip planned in 2 minutes

Three prompts that build on each other to get a trip plan that fits you: a day-by-day table with plan B, a packing list based on weather and activities – and an honest list of what to check before you go.

How it works

  1. Collect your details. Destination, days, budget on site, pace, interests, who's coming, where you're staying and what you definitely don't want. The more honest you are about your pace, the better the plan fits.
  2. Get the plan. Prompt 1 with your details. Answer Claude's follow-up questions briefly – then you get the day plan as a table with plan B and a budget estimate.
  3. Add the packing list. Prompt 2 in the same chat. Claude already knows your plan and packs for your actual activities instead of a generic list.
  4. Work through the check list. Check opening hours, closing days, prices and timetables on the official websites before you travel. If web search is turned on in your Claude, it can help you look things up – still click the source yourself.
  5. Adjust on the road. Rain, a closed museum or tired legs? Prompt 3 in the same chat – Claude only rebuilds the days that are affected.

Prompt 1: The trip plan as a table

You are my trip planner. Plan a trip that fits me – not the standard tourist checklist.

My details:
- Destination: ___
- Travel dates / number of days: ___
- Budget on site (excluding flights/travel there): ___
- Pace: ___ (relaxed / medium / packed)
- Interests: ___ (e.g. food, culture, nature, beach, nightlife)
- Who's coming: ___ (solo, couple, family with kids aged ___)
- Where I'm staying: ___
- Things I definitely don't want: ___

If an important detail is missing, ask me first (max. 3 questions) before you plan.

Then deliver:
1. DAY PLAN as a table: Day | Morning | Afternoon | Evening | Travel time between stops. Max. 3 activities per day, close to each other, matching my pace. Build in breaks.
2. One short sentence per activity on why it fits my interests.
3. BUDGET CHECK: rough range per day for food, tickets and local transport – clearly marked as an estimate.
4. PLAN B: for every day with outdoor activities, an alternative for rain or heat.
5. CHECK BEFORE YOU GO: a list of everything you don't know for sure (opening hours, closing days, prices, timetables, reservations needed, seasonal closures).

Rules: Don't invent addresses, prices or opening hours. If you're not sure about something, put it on the "check before you go" list instead of guessing.

Before the trip, as soon as destination and days are fixed.

Prompt 2: Packing list that matches the plan

Make me a packing list for the trip plan above.

- Travel season and the weather I expect: ___ (if I don't know: tell me what's typical there at this time of year and mark it as an estimate)
- Luggage: ___ (carry-on only / checked bag)
- Special plans: ___ (e.g. hike, nice dinner, beach, kids)

Group the list into: Documents & money | Clothes (matching the activities in the plan) | Tech | Health | Extras for plan B.
After each item, add in brackets which day/activity I need it for. Remove anything I can easily buy there and list it separately under "Can buy there".
At the end: the 3 things people forget most often on a trip like this.

In the same chat, a few days before you leave.

Prompt 3: Rebuild the plan on the road

I'm on site now and checked the plan. This has changed: ___ (e.g. the museum is closed today, it's raining, we're more tired than expected, budget is tighter).

Adjust the rest of the plan:
1. Show only the days that change, as a new table (Day | Morning | Afternoon | Evening).
2. Keep the pace and the distances short – rather one activity less.
3. Mark new activities I should check again before going.
4. Tell me in one sentence what I miss because of the change and whether it's worth doing on another day.

On site, when something changes.

Example

Lisbon, 3 days, around $650 on site, relaxed pace, into food and views, travelling as a couple. Claude asks whether you're fine walking uphill, then delivers: Day 1 old town – food hall – fado bar, Day 2 museum – beach – viewpoint at sunset, Day 3 day trip – picnic – rooftop bar. Plan B for day 2: museum and café instead of the beach. Packing list: rain jacket (day 2), comfy shoes (hilly old town), sunscreen. Check before you go: the museum's closing day, train times for the day trip, whether the bar needs a reservation.

Check before you send

  • Max. 3 activities per day – does that match your pace?
  • All prices are marked as estimates, none as fixed prices.
  • Every opening time and timetable is on the "check before you go" list.
  • Every outdoor day has a plan B.

Fill in destination, days, budget, pace and interests – Claude asks a few quick questions and returns a day-by-day table with a plan B and a list of what to check before you go.

Guide: Understand and answer any official letter

Use three prompts to turn a confusing official letter into a clear overview (deadline first), get the reply drafted with an enclosure list and – if needed – prepare a phone call with the sender.

How it works

  1. Prepare the letter. Photograph or scan every page (including the back – that's often where deadlines and your options to appeal are). Black out ID numbers, tax numbers, account numbers and similar first.
  2. Get the plain-English version. Send prompt 1 with today's date and the letter. Read the deadline first and check it against the quoted line in the original – then put it in your calendar right away.
  3. Collect the documents. Tick off the list from point 3. If something is missing, note by when you can send it.
  4. Write the reply. Prompt 2 in the same chat. Check the draft, fill the gaps, add the enclosures and send it the way the letter asks (mail, email, portal). Keep a copy and the date you sent it.
  5. Unsure? Call. If you don't understand something, disagree, or the deadline is getting tight: prompt 3 for a call cheat sheet. For appeals, repayment demands or disputes, ask a free advice service, a tax advisor or a lawyer.

Prompt 1: Plain English, deadline first

I got an official letter (photo or text below) – from a government agency, tax office, court, insurer, bank or similar. Translate it into plain English for me.

Answer in exactly this order:
1. DEADLINE FIRST: Is there a deadline? Give the date or how it's calculated (e.g. "within 30 days of the date of this notice") and quote the line from the letter. If there's no deadline, say so explicitly.
2. Who is writing to me and why – in 2–3 sentences, no jargon.
3. What do they want from me? As a list: documents, information, payments – each with where it says so in the letter.
4. What happens if I do nothing? Only what the letter itself says.
5. My next steps as a checklist I can tick off.
6. Up to 5 difficult terms from the letter, briefly explained.
7. When I should call them or get advice instead (e.g. if I don't understand something, disagree, am asked to pay money back, or the deadline is tight).

Rules: Don't add anything that isn't in the letter. If something is unreadable or ambiguous, write "unclear – check the original". Explain only – no legal or tax advice.

Today's date: ___
My letter: ___

As soon as the letter arrives – before anything else.

Prompt 2: Reply to the sender

Now write my reply to the sender (max. 150 words, polite and to the point):
- From: ___ (name, address). Recipient and reference number exactly as they appear in the letter.
- Subject line with the reference number and the date of the letter.
- I'm enclosing: ___ (list of documents I'm sending).
- If something is missing or will come later: ___ (reason + by when).
- Ask for a short confirmation of receipt.
Below it: a list of enclosures and a note on how the letter says I should reply (mail, email or online portal). Where information is missing, put ___ instead of making something up.

In the same chat, once you have the documents together.

Prompt 3: Cheat sheet for the phone call

I want to call them before I reply. Make me a cheat sheet for the call:
1. The 3–5 questions that are still open after reading my letter – short and clear.
2. What I should have ready (reference number, date of the letter, documents).
3. One opening sentence ("Hi, I'm calling about …").
4. A mini template for notes: date, time, name of the person, what was agreed.
Finally, tell me whether I should ask for any extension to be confirmed in writing.

When questions are still open or you need more time.

Example

Sam gets a two-page letter from the housing benefit office. Prompt 1 shows at the top: deadline Dec 2 (with the quoted line), they want bank statements for the last three months and a proof of address. The landlord has to sign the proof of address first – so prompt 3: a cheat sheet for calling to ask whether it can follow a few days later. Then prompt 2 writes the reply with an enclosure list.

Check before you send

  • Deadline checked in the original and in your calendar?
  • Sensitive numbers blacked out before uploading?
  • Reply sent with reference number, enclosure list and via the right channel – copy kept?
  • Disagree or asked to pay money back? Get advice before you reply.

Paste a photo or the text of the letter and add today's date – Claude gives you the deadline first, then what they want and which documents, each with where it says so in the letter.

Guide: Never say "write more human" again – make Claude sound like you

Turn 2–3 of your own texts into a style profile, ban typical AI phrases, and have every draft rewritten so it sounds like you.

How it works

  1. Collect samples. Find 2–3 texts you wrote yourself – ideally from the same channel as your target text (email for email, post for post). Remove names, numbers and anything confidential first.
  2. Style profile + text in one go. Send prompt 1. Claude derives up to 8 style rules with supporting quotes, rewrites your text without clichés and marks sentences that still sound generic.
  3. Correct the profile. Is a rule wrong? Say so directly ("I never write 'Hi all'", "Emojis only at the end"). The more accurate the profile, the better every following text.
  4. Check every new draft. Use prompt 2 for anything that might sound like AI – including texts from other tools. You get a table: Sentence | Problem | Better.
  5. Save the profile. Prompt 3 turns it into a block of at most 150 words. Save it as a note and paste it into new chats – or into the instructions of a Claude project.

Prompt 1: Style profile + text in your voice

You're my ghostwriter. Goal: texts that sound like I wrote them myself – not like AI.

Here are 2–3 texts I wrote myself (emails, posts, messages):
<sample1>___</sample1>
<sample2>___</sample2>
<sample3>___ (optional)</sample3>

Text you should write or rewrite in my style: ___
Purpose and reader: ___ (e.g. email to a client, LinkedIn post, message to my team)

How to work:
1. From my samples, derive a style profile with at most 8 rules: typical sentence length, how I start sentences, favorite words and phrases, how I address people (first name, "Hi", "Hey"…), tone (e.g. direct, casual, dry), punctuation and emojis, and what I never do. Back up each rule with a short quote from my samples.
2. Ban list: don't use typical AI phrases like "In today's fast-paced world", "It's important to note", "an exciting journey", "Let's dive in", "seamless", "holistic", "game-changer", "In conclusion" – and no words that would never appear in my samples. No groups of three just for rhythm, no rhetorical question as the opener.
3. Write the text in my style. Content and facts stay the same – don't add anything.
4. Check: afterwards, mark every sentence that still sounds generic or AI-like with [GENERIC] and suggest a version in my voice.

Output: 1) style profile as a list, 2) finished text, 3) marked sentences with alternatives.

Once with your samples – gives you the profile, the finished text and the flags.

Prompt 2: AI-phrase check for every draft

Check this draft against my style profile and the ban list from above:
___ (paste the draft – yours, Claude's or from another tool)

1. Mark every sentence that breaks a rule or contains a phrase from the ban list, and name the rule.
2. Give an alternative in my voice for each marked sentence.
3. Finish with one sentence: what makes this draft sound most like AI?

Don't change the content. Output as a table: Sentence | Problem | Better.

Afterwards in the same chat, for every new draft.

Prompt 3: Reusable style block

Turn my style profile and the ban list into a compact block (max. 150 words) that I can paste at the start of new chats or into the instructions of a Claude project.
- Write each rule as a clear instruction ("Write …", "Avoid …") with a mini example from my samples.
- Include the full ban list.
- End with: "If you're unsure about tone or content, ask me instead of guessing."

Once the profile fits – so you never have to start from scratch.

Example

Maya sends a newsletter for her café. As samples she uses two old Instagram posts and an email to regulars. Claude's profile: short sentences, always first names, likes to start with "Quick version:", emojis only at the end. "In today's fast-paced world, we're excited to bring you engaging content" becomes "Quick version: the newsletter now comes out every Friday. Have a look! ☕" – and the check flags one sentence that still sounds like a brochure.

Check before you send

  • Are the samples really yours (not already AI-edited)?
  • Does every style rule have a supporting quote – and is it right?
  • Are facts, names and dates in the final text unchanged and correct?
  • Stay honest: the prompt makes texts more personal – it's not a trick to fool AI detectors.

Paste 2–3 of your own texts as samples, add the target text and the reader – Claude gives you your style profile, the text in your voice, and flags what still sounds generic.

GuideReel E008

Guide: Rehearse your raise with Claude – arguments, role-play, feedback, plan B

You walk into your raise conversation prepared: three arguments from your own work, practised answers to the usual objections, and a plan B if the answer isn't yes.

How it works

  1. Collect your number and facts. Research your target number yourself (industry salary surveys, official wage statistics like the US BLS or UK ONS, your pay scale). Add bullet points: what you achieved or took on in the last 12 months.
  2. Build your arguments. Send Prompt 1. Claude asks where proof is missing and gives you 3 arguments, an opening line, the sentence with your target number and the 5 most likely objections.
  3. Dry run. Prompt 2 in the same chat: Claude plays your manager and brings the objections one at a time. Answer the way you'd really say it. Type "Stop" to end the role-play.
  4. Get feedback and go again. Prompt 3 shows what convinced, what was too vague, and gives you better lines. Then do a second round with tougher objections.
  5. Prepare plan B. Prompt 4: alternatives if the answer is no, a sentence to lock in a follow-up date, and the email after the meeting.

Prompt 1: Arguments, opening line, objections

You're my salary negotiation coach. I'm preparing a conversation with my manager about a raise.

My situation:
- Job / role: ___
- How long I've been at the company / in this role: ___
- Current salary (gross per year): ___
- My target (gross per year): ___ – source for this number: ___ (e.g. salary surveys for my industry, official wage statistics like the US BLS or UK ONS, a pay scale or collective agreement)
- What I achieved or took on in the last 12 months (bullet points): ___
- What my manager is like (e.g. numbers-driven, avoids conflict, always short on time): ___

How to work:
1. First ask me up to 3 questions if something important is missing (e.g. measurable results). Wait for my answers.
2. Turn my bullet points into my 3 strongest arguments – each with: what I did, what it meant for the team or company, and proof (a result, a number, praise). Only use what I told you, don't invent anything.
3. Write an opening line (2 sentences max) to start the conversation, and the sentence I use to name my target number.
4. List the 5 objections I'm most likely to hear in my situation.
5. Don't invent market salaries. If my target looks weakly backed by my arguments, tell me honestly.

A few days before the conversation, first.

Prompt 2: Role-play: Claude plays your manager

Now let's practice. You play my manager in the raise conversation – friendly, but not easy to convince. Use the objections from above and react realistically to what I say.

Rules:
- Only ever say one sentence or question, then wait for my answer.
- Bring the objections one at a time, not all at once. If my answer is weak, push back.
- Don't give in just because I'm polite – only to good arguments.
- Stay in character until I type "Stop". No feedback during the role-play.
- Tone between us: ___ (first names and casual / formal).

Start with: "Good to see you. What's on your mind?"

Right after, in the same chat – as often as you like.

Prompt 3: Honest feedback + better lines

Stop. Step out of the role. Now give me honest feedback on my answers in the role-play:
1. What was convincing? (max. 3 points, each with a quote from my answers)
2. Where was I too vague, too defensive, or did I apologize? (with quotes)
3. For the 3 weakest moments: one better line each that I can say word for word.
4. Did I state my target number clearly? If not: the sentence for it.
5. One thing I should do differently in the next round.

Then ask me if we should do another round – this time with tougher objections.

After every round, once you've typed "Stop".

Prompt 4: Plan B if the answer is no

Let's say my manager says no or "not right now". Prepare me for that:
1. 5 alternatives I can negotiate for instead, matched to my situation (e.g. training budget, extra days off, remote days, a bonus, more responsibility or a new title) – with one sentence each on how to bring it up.
2. One sentence to lock in a fixed follow-up date (e.g. in 3 or 6 months), plus clear criteria for what a raise will be measured on then.
3. A short, friendly email after the meeting that puts in writing what we agreed.
4. Signs that it's worth quietly looking around elsewhere at the same time – factual, no drama.

At the end of your prep – and after the meeting for the email.

Example

Example (invented): Lena is a project manager, three years on the team, has led a client project on her own since March, onboarded two new teammates and got written praise from the client. Prompt 1 turns that into three arguments and the opener "I'd like to talk about my salary, because my role has changed a lot since March." In the dry run she hears "There's just no budget this year" – and practises her answer with the client project. Prompt 3 shows: "I just want more" was too vague; the concrete number with a reason works better. Prompt 4 gives her plan B: training now and a follow-up in six months with fixed criteria.

Check before you send

  • Target number researched yourself – Claude doesn't invent salaries.
  • At least two rounds of role-play done.
  • Opening line and the sentence with your number practised out loud once.
  • Plan B and follow-up date written down before you walk in.

Fill in your role, salary, target number with its source, and your wins – Claude builds arguments, an opening line and the objections you then practise in the role-play.

Guide: Get your data back – access, deletion, reminder

Use four prompts to get finished privacy letters from Claude: access request, deletion request, a reminder once the deadline has passed and, if needed, a complaint to the regulator – you send them yourself.

How it works

  1. Find the contact. The company's privacy policy names where privacy requests go (often a dedicated email address or a data protection officer).
  2. Request access. Prompt 1 with the company, your name, how they know you (email, customer number) and where you live. Check the letter, send it, note the date you sent it.
  3. Get it deleted. Want out? Prompt 2 in the same chat. It also asks about retention duties – some data (e.g. invoices) a company may have to keep for a while, but it has to tell you why.
  4. Watch the deadline. GDPR/UK GDPR: at the latest one month (extendable by two more months with reasons). CCPA: 45 days (extendable by 45 more). No answer? Prompt 3 writes the reminder.
  5. If nothing happens. Prompt 4 prepares a complaint to the data protection authority (EU national authority, the ICO in the UK, the California Privacy Protection Agency). If there's a dispute: ask a consumer advice service or a lawyer.

Prompt 1: Access request (GDPR Art. 15 / CCPA)

You help me use my right of access to my personal data. Write me a finished, factual request I can send by email or letter.

My details:
- Company: ___ (name + privacy contact from their privacy policy)
- My name: ___
- How the company knows me: ___ (e.g. email address, customer number, phone number)
- Where I live: ___ (EU/EEA → GDPR · UK → UK GDPR · California → CCPA/CPRA)
- Today's date: ___

The request should:
1. Use the subject line "Subject access request under Article 15 GDPR" (UK: "… UK GDPR"; California: "Request to know under the CCPA").
2. Ask them to confirm whether they process personal data about me and to send me a copy of that data.
3. Ask for: the purposes, the categories of data, who they shared it with, how long they keep it, where they got it from, and whether automated decisions or profiling are used.
4. State the deadline: GDPR/UK GDPR – without undue delay and at the latest within one month of receipt (Art. 12(3)); CCPA – within 45 calendar days.
5. Be polite but firm, max. 200 words, no threats.
6. Below the request: the approximate date the deadline ends, and a short list of what I should send to prove who I am (only as much as necessary).

Don't invent any data about me, addresses or reference numbers – wherever something is missing, put ___.

When you want to know what a company has stored about you.

Prompt 2: Deletion request (GDPR Art. 17 / CCPA)

Now, with the same details, write me a deletion request (max. 180 words, factual):
- Subject: "Request for erasure of my personal data (Article 17 GDPR)" (UK: UK GDPR; California: "Request to delete under the CCPA").
- Ask them to delete all personal data about me without undue delay and to confirm the deletion in writing.
- If they have to keep some data because of a legal obligation: ask them to tell me which data, on what basis and until when.
- Objection to marketing by email/post (GDPR Art. 21(2)) / opt-out of sale or sharing (CCPA): ___ (yes/no).
- Ask them to inform anyone they shared my data with about the deletion (GDPR Art. 19).
- Same deadline as in the first request.
Wherever details are missing: ___ instead of inventing anything.

In the same chat, when you want your data gone (works without an access request first, too).

Prompt 3: Reminder after the deadline

My request (___ access / deletion) of ___ to ___ has gone unanswered past the deadline. They ___ (did not / did on ___) tell me they were extending the deadline.

Write me a short, firm reminder (max. 120 words):
1. Refer to my original request with its date.
2. Point out that the legal deadline has passed (GDPR/UK GDPR: one month, Art. 12(3); CCPA: 45 days). If they told me about a valid extension (GDPR: up to two more months; CCPA: up to 45 more days, with reasons), don't write the reminder – tell me until when I should still wait.
3. New deadline: 14 days from today (today is ___).
4. A factual note that otherwise I can complain to the data protection authority (EU: my national authority, Art. 77 GDPR; UK: the ICO; California: the California Privacy Protection Agency).

Below that, tell me which authority is probably responsible and mark it as something I have to double-check myself.

When the deadline has passed without an answer.

Prompt 4: Prepare a complaint

The company ___ did not reply (or only partly: ___) to my request of ___ and my reminder of ___.
Help me prepare a complaint to the data protection authority:
1. A factual summary as a timeline with all dates.
2. A list of documents I should attach (request, reminder, proof of sending, their reply if any).
3. A short complaint text (max. 200 words).
Don't add anything that isn't true, and tell me where I'd be better off asking a consumer advice service or a lawyer.

When even the reminder gets no response.

Example

Lisa keeps getting marketing emails from an online shop she ordered from once, years ago. Prompt 1 with the shop's name, her name and her email gives her a finished access request plus the date the deadline ends. Then Prompt 2 with "objection to marketing: yes" – the shop should delete her data and say what it still has to keep for legal reasons. If nothing comes back in time, Prompt 3 writes the reminder with a new 14-day deadline.

Check before you send

  • Is the request going to the company's actual privacy contact?
  • Only sent the details needed to identify you?
  • Noted the date you sent it and put the deadline in your calendar?
  • Claude writes the letters but isn't legal advice – in a dispute, ask a consumer advice service or a lawyer.

Fill in the company, your name, how they know you and where you live – Claude writes the finished access request (GDPR, UK GDPR or CCPA) with the deadline; you send it yourself.

GuideReel E006

Guide: Let Claude take your plan apart – before reality does

Get honest, structured criticism of your own idea or plan – with fixes and tests instead of just agreement.

How it works

  1. Write down your idea and goal. 2–5 sentences on the idea (what, for whom, how, effort) and one concrete goal. The sharper the goal, the sharper the criticism.
  2. Start the critical reviewer. Send prompt 1. If something's missing, Claude asks first, then delivers 5 weak spots ranked by risk, a fix for each and 3 assumptions with a test.
  3. Check the criticism. Prompt 2: Claude names where it might be wrong itself, collects the real arguments in favour and works the best fixes into your plan.
  4. Pre-mortem. Prompt 3: Claude imagines the plan has failed – and derives early warning signs and a launch checklist from that.
  5. Decide for yourself. Claude doesn't know your market better than you do. Take the tests from step 2 and check the most important assumption in the real world before you invest money or a lot of time.

Prompt 1: Critical reviewer: 5 weak spots + fixes

Be my critical reviewer, not my fan. I don't want validation – I want an honest analysis of the weak spots.

My idea / plan: ___ (2–5 sentences: what, for whom, how, with what budget/time)
My goal: ___ (how I'll know it worked – as concrete as possible)
What I already know or have tested: ___ (or "nothing yet")

Here's how to do it:
