Guide: Plan your whole week of dinners with Claude

In one flow, get a 7-night meal plan, a grocery list sorted by store section and a leftovers plan that fit your actual life.

How it works

  1. Collect your facts. People, time per night, budget, hard no's and what's still in the fridge – the save slide helps you fill them in.
  2. Get the plan. Send prompt 1 and answer Claude's questions. You get 7 nights as a table. Don't like a dish? "Swap Wednesday for something with rice."
  3. Build the grocery list. Prompt 2: a list sorted by store section, quantities added up, staples listed separately to tick off.
  4. Prep and leftovers. Prompt 3 on your prep day, prompt 4 whenever something is left over.

Prompt 1: Weekly meal plan

You're my weekly meal planner. First ask me these questions and wait for my answers before you plan anything:
1. How many people are you cooking for (adults/kids)?
2. How much time do you have to cook each night?
3. Roughly what's your budget for the week (and in which currency)?
4. What's a hard no (allergies, intolerances, foods nobody eats)?
5. What's already in the fridge or pantry that needs to be used up?
6. How many nights do you want to cook, and which nights are you eating out?

Then create:
- A table with 7 nights: day – dish – cooking time – main ingredients.
- Plan it so ingredients get used more than once (e.g. Monday's vegetables go into Tuesday's wraps) and as little as possible is left over.
- At most one more elaborate dish per week, everything else within my time limit.
- Use what I already have at home first.

My answers: ___

Once a week, e.g. on Sunday.

Prompt 2: Grocery list by aisle

Turn the meal plan into a grocery list:
1. Sorted by store section: produce, dairy & eggs, meat/fish, bakery, pantry & canned goods, frozen, household/other.
2. Quantities for all dishes added up, in units you can actually buy (e.g. "zucchini ×2", "rice 1 kg / 2 lb").
3. A separate "You probably have this already" list (oil, salt, spices) to tick off.
4. Anything I said I already have at home stays off the list.
5. At the end, a rough estimate of whether this fits my budget of ___, plus 2 money-saving tips if it doesn't. Mention that prices vary by store and region.

Right after the plan.

Prompt 3: Meal prep + leftovers plan

Plan my meal prep and leftovers:
1. What can I prepare on ___ (e.g. Sunday) in max. 60 minutes so the weeknights go faster? As a step-by-step list in a sensible order.
2. Which ingredients will probably be left over at the end of the week? Suggest a way to use each one.
3. Roughly how long does each prepped dish keep in the fridge, and what should I freeze instead? Add a note that I should double-check food safety myself if in doubt.

When you want to plan a prep day.

Prompt 4: Leftover rescue

I've still got: ___ (paste your leftovers).
I have ___ minutes. Suggest 3 dishes that use almost only these ingredients – each with an ingredient list, what I'd need to buy extra (max. 2 things) and short instructions in 5 steps. Keep my hard no's from above in mind.

During the week, whenever ingredients are left over.

Example

Two adults and one kid, max. 30 minutes per night, no mushrooms, feta and zucchini already in the fridge. Claude plans roast veggies with feta on Monday, uses the leftover vegetables for wraps on Tuesday, lentil bolognese on Wednesday and egg fried rice on Thursday – and the grocery list only includes the feta and zucchini you're still missing.

Check before you send

  • Check every hard no and allergy in the plan yourself – don't just trust Claude.
  • Prices are rough estimates only: compare with your own store.
  • At least two dishes share ingredients.
  • If in doubt, double-check how long prepped food keeps.

Send the prompt, answer Claude's 6 questions – then you get the plan as a table.

Guide: Sort your inbox in 10 minutes

Turn a full inbox into a clear order: what's due today, ready-made reply drafts for the most urgent emails and a list of everything that can wait.

How it works

  1. Collect your emails. Copy 5–15 emails with sender, date, subject and text. Black out or leave out anything confidential first (passwords, account numbers, health data).
  2. Let Claude sort them. Send Prompt 1 with today's date and your current priorities. You get a table with deadlines and traffic-light priority, three reply drafts and a "can wait" list.
  3. Fill the gaps and reply. Wherever a draft says ___, Claude is missing information. Give it with Prompt 2 and get a finished reply plus a short version.
  4. Schedule the rest. Prompt 3 spreads the "can wait" emails over your week and shows what you can archive or unsubscribe from.

Prompt 1: Inbox sorter: table + 3 drafts

I'm pasting ___ emails from my inbox below (5–15, each with sender, date, subject and text). Help me work through them in the right order.

Today is ___ (date). What matters most to me right now: ___ (e.g. a project, specific clients, family).

Here's how to do it:
1. Make a table with these columns: No. | Sender | What it's about (max. 8 words) | What do they expect from me? | Deadline | Priority (🔴 today / 🟡 this week / 🟢 can wait).
2. Deadline: only use deadlines that are actually written in the email, and quote the line briefly. If there's none, write "❓ no deadline given" – don't estimate or make anything up.
3. Sort the table by priority, and by deadline within the same priority.
4. For the 3 most urgent emails, write a short reply draft each (max. 5 sentences, matching the tone of the original email). Where you're missing information, put ___ as a gap instead of inventing something.
5. Below that, under "Can wait", list every email that doesn't need a reply from me or has time – with one sentence on why.
6. At the end, ask me up to 3 questions if anything is missing for the prioritisation.

Here are the emails:
<emails>
___
</emails>

When your inbox is full and you don't know where to start.

Prompt 2: Finish a reply

Take the reply draft for email no. ___ from your table.

My info for the gaps: ___
Tone: ___ (casual / friendly-professional / brief)

Finish the reply (max. 120 words) and also give me a short version of 3 sentences max. Only use facts from the email or from my info – if something is still missing, leave ___ in place and tell me what you still need.

After Prompt 1, for each of the urgent emails.

Prompt 3: Schedule the "can wait" list

Here's my "Can wait" list from before: ___

This week I have about ___ minutes a day for email.
1. Spread these emails over the next few days as a table: Day | Email | What to do | Estimated minutes (your estimate, marked as such).
2. Mark which emails I can probably archive without replying, and why.
3. Name senders (e.g. newsletters) where unsubscribing or a filter might be worth it.

Once the urgent stuff is done.

Example

Monday morning, 12 emails: Sara from your team needs the slides by tomorrow (🔴), a client asks about an invoice and wants an answer by Friday (🔴), your boss writes "Got a minute today?" (🟡), your landlord wants to book a heating check but gives no date (❓ no deadline given). Newsletters and shipping updates go on the "can wait" list. Claude drafts replies for Sara, the client and your boss – Sara's with the gap "by ___ pm".

Check before you send

  • Are the deadlines really written that way in the emails (check the quote)?
  • Have you filled every ___ gap before sending?
  • Nothing confidential pasted that doesn't belong there?
  • Read every reply yourself before you hit send?

Paste 5–15 emails at the bottom, add today's date and your priorities – Claude gives you a table, 3 reply drafts and a "can wait" list.

Guide: Any awkward message – clear and fair

Say no, cancel or set a boundary with a message that's clear, sounds fair and leaves no back door open.

How it works

  1. Collect three inputs. What's the situation, who gets the message (and how do you know each other), what do you want to achieve? Add the channel: a text reads differently from an email.
  2. Get three versions. Send Prompt 1. If something's missing, Claude asks first. Then you get friendly, clear and short – plus the sentence you should skip.
  3. Polish with a reality check. Prompt 2: pick a version, adjust the tone and let Claude read it from the recipient's side. That shows where it still sounds like "yes, maybe".
  4. Send it – and stick to it. If the reply pushes back or guilt-trips you, Prompt 3 helps you stay kind and firm, or offer a real compromise.

Prompt 1: 3 versions + the never-send line

I need to write an awkward message and want to stay clear but fair.

Situation: ___ (what happened / what is being asked of me?)
Recipient: ___ (who is it and what's our relationship – e.g. my manager, a coworker, a friend, a client)
My goal: ___ (e.g. say no, cancel, set a boundary – and what should stay good afterwards)
Channel: ___ (text message, email, Slack …)

Here's how to do it:
1. If any of this is missing or unclear, first ask me up to 3 short questions and wait for my answer.
2. Then write 3 versions of the message:
   – FRIENDLY: warm, with thanks or understanding, but still unmistakable.
   – CLEAR: matter-of-fact and direct, with a concrete alternative if there is one.
   – SHORT: 2 sentences max.
3. Rules for all versions: the no (or cancellation) comes in the first or second sentence. Don't invent excuses, don't over-apologise, and avoid anything that leaves a back door open ("maybe", "actually", "we'll see"). Match the channel and our relationship (formal or casual).
4. Then give me the one sentence I should never send in this situation and explain in one sentence why it backfires.
5. Finish with one sentence on which version you'd recommend here and why.

As soon as you notice you're putting a message off.

Prompt 2: Polish + recipient check

I'll take version ___. Adjust it like this:
– Tone: ___ (e.g. a bit warmer / more direct / less formal)
– Must stay in: ___
– Take out: ___

Then read the message once from the point of view of ___ (recipient) and tell me honestly:
1. How will it most likely land?
2. Is there any part that could be misread or still sounds like a "yes"?
Then give me the final version, ready to copy.

After Prompt 1, before you hit send.

Prompt 3: Handle the reply

I sent the message. Here's the reply:
<reply>
___ (paste the reply here)
</reply>

Help me hold my ground:
1. Sum up in one sentence what the other person actually wants.
2. Write 2 short replies: one that kindly sticks to my no, and one with a compromise I can genuinely offer: ___ (or "none").
3. If the reply puts pressure on me or tries to guilt-trip me, tell me plainly and write a calm reply that doesn't take the bait.

When the other person pushes back or applies pressure.

Example

Your manager texts on Friday evening: "Can you cover this Saturday?" You don't want to, but you get along well. Input: situation = manager asks me to cover Saturday, recipient = my manager, good relationship, goal = say no without tension afterwards, channel = text message. Claude returns e.g. CLEAR: "I can't do Saturday. Happy to help first thing Monday." – and warns you against "I don't really have plans, but …", because it invites negotiation.

Check before you send

  • The no is in the first or second sentence.
  • No invented excuse, no triple apology.
  • No "maybe" or "actually" that still sounds like a yes.
  • The tone fits the channel and the relationship.

Fill in the situation, recipient and goal – Claude gives you three versions (friendly, clear, short) plus the line you should never send.

Guide: Claude as your personal tutor – from your material to a mock exam

Your own study material becomes a plan up to exam day – and Claude quizzes you, explains gaps simply and tests you with a mock exam at the end, instead of you just reading summaries.

How it works

  1. Add your material. Upload or paste your notes, slides or table of contents. The more precise the material, the better the plan and the questions fit.
  2. Build the study plan. Send prompt 1 with the date, subject and your time per day. Claude asks what you already know, then builds the plan with reviews and a buffer.
  3. Get quizzed every day. Instead of rereading: prompt 2 with the topic of the day. Answer yourself first, then you get the solution. Mistakes come back later.
  4. Close the gaps. Anything that doesn't stick: prompt 3 – first explained super simply, then with the technical terms – and explain it back in your own words.
  5. Take a mock exam. Two days before: prompt 4. Questions without answers, grading with points, the 3 most important topics to review.

Prompt 1: Study plan up to exam day

You're my study coach. I have an exam in ___ (subject) on ___ (date). Today is ___. I can study about ___ minutes a day, and not at all on these days: ___.

<material>
___ (paste the table of contents, your notes or slides – or upload them as a file)
</material>

How to work:
1. Split the material into topic blocks and estimate how big each one is (small / medium / large) – based only on what's in the material.
2. Briefly ask me which topics I already know well and where I'm unsure. Wait for my answer.
3. Then build a study plan as a table: Date – Topic – What I do (learn new / get quizzed / review) – Duration.
4. Schedule reviews at growing intervals (e.g. after 1, 3 and 7 days) and keep the last 2 days before the exam free for a mock exam and gaps.
5. Stick to my time per day. If it isn't enough, tell me honestly what I should shorten or drop.

Once at the start, as soon as you know the exam date.

Prompt 2: Quiz mode

Quiz me on ___ (topic) now – like a strict but friendly tutor.
Rules:
- Only ONE question at a time, then wait for my answer.
- Only after my answer: correct / partly / wrong, the right answer in 2–3 sentences and what I was missing.
- Mix the question types: explain a term, give an example, the difference between two things, a small application task.
- Anything I got wrong, ask again later in a different way.
- After 10 questions: a short review – what I know, what I need to go over again.
Only use content from my material. If something isn't in it, don't ask about it.

Every study day, in the same chat as prompt 1 (so Claude knows your material).

Prompt 3: Explain it like I'm five

I don't understand ___. Explain it in three levels:
1. Like I'm five – with a comparison from everyday life.
2. A bit more precise, with the correct technical terms (each term explained in one sentence).
3. The way it could come up in the exam.
Then ask me one check question and I'll explain it back to you in my own words. Tell me what's still wrong or vague in my explanation.

Whenever something doesn't stick during the quiz.

Prompt 4: Mock exam with grading

Make me a mock exam on ___ from my material, about as long as the real one (___ minutes, question types: ___).
- First give me only the questions, no answers.
- I'll send you my answers. Then grade every question with points and a short reason.
- Finally: the 3 topics I still need to review before the exam, each with one practice question.

1–2 days before the exam.

Example

Biology exam in 12 days, 45 minutes a day: Claude splits your notes into topic blocks, asks about your weak spots and plans cell biology, genetics and metabolism with reviews. During the quiz you miss the keyword ATP for "mitochondria" – Claude explains it with a power-plant comparison, asks again two days later and puts it into the mock exam.

Check before you send

  • Does the plan fit your real time per day – even on busy days?
  • When you get quizzed, do you really answer yourself before reading the solution?
  • Do the facts match your own material? If in doubt, check the original – Claude can make mistakes.
  • Mock exam planned 1–2 days before?

Fill in exam date, subject, study time and your material – Claude asks a quick question and builds your study plan up to exam day.

Guide: Turn every meeting into a finished to-do list

Messy meeting notes or a transcript become a clear task list with who, what and by when – no invented names or deadlines – plus a ready-to-send follow-up email.

How it works

  1. Collect your notes. Right after the meeting, copy everything together: your bullet points, chat messages, the transcript. Messy is fine.
  2. Build the list. Send prompt 1. Claude builds the table What – Who – By when, lists decisions and open questions, and marks gaps with "❓ missing".
  3. Close the gaps. Answer Claude's questions and send prompt 2. Claude sorts everything by deadline and gives each person their own list.
  4. Send the email. Send prompt 3, pick your tone, read it over once and send it to everyone – plus a 3-line version for the team chat.

Prompt 1: Pull out the tasks

Here are my notes from a meeting. They're messy and incomplete.

<notes>
___ (paste your notes or the transcript here)
</notes>

Meeting: ___ (topic, date, who was there)

Turn this into a clear task list. Work like this:
1. Read everything and collect every task, every decision and every open question – even if it was only mentioned in passing.
2. Tasks as a table: No. – What exactly needs to be done (start with a verb) – Who – By when – Source (short quote from the notes).
3. Don't invent names or deadlines. If something isn't in the notes, write "❓ missing" in that cell.
4. Below the table: "Decisions" as a short list (what was agreed?).
5. Below that: "Open questions" – everything that still needs to be clarified.
6. Finally: ask me one concrete question for every "❓ missing", numbered, so I can answer quickly.

Right after the meeting, with all your notes.

Prompt 2: Close the gaps & sort

Here are my answers to your questions:
___ (e.g. 1. Tom calls the client, by Wednesday. 2. Budget: still open.)

Update the task list with them:
- Add the answers to the table.
- Anything still unclear stays "❓ missing".
- Sort by deadline, most urgent first.
- Mark tasks that depend on another one with "⏳ waiting for No. X".
- Give me a short list for each person: "Name: task – by when".

When Claude has asked you questions.

Prompt 3: Follow-up email

Now write the follow-up email to everyone who was in the meeting.

- Subject: short, with the meeting topic and date.
- Tone: ___ (e.g. casual and friendly / formal)
- Structure: 1 sentence of thanks → decisions (bullet points) → tasks per person with deadline → open questions with a request to reply by ___ → short sign-off.
- Max 150 words, no filler phrases.
- Only use what's in the task list above, don't add anything.
Then also give me a 3-line version for the team chat (Slack, Teams or similar).

Once the list is done – ideally the same day.

Example

Product launch meeting: paste the notes → prompt 1 gives "Lisa – send offer – Fri", "Tom – update slides – Mon" and "❓ missing – call client – ❓ missing" plus the question "Who calls the client, and by when?" → answer "Ana, Wednesday" → prompt 2 sorts everything by deadline → prompt 3 writes the email to the team.

Check before you send

  • Every task has a person and a deadline – or is clearly marked "❓ missing"
  • Nothing in the email that wasn't discussed in the meeting
  • Check confidential content before pasting (follow your company's rules)

Paste your notes or transcript at the top – Claude builds the Who/What/By when table and asks instead of inventing gaps.

Guide: Analyze long documents with Claude

For contracts, reports, minutes and PDFs: Claude only tells you what's actually in the text – with proof.

How it works

  1. Paste the document. Copy the full text into the chat or upload the file – at the very top, before anything else.
  2. Get the overview first. Send prompt 1: Claude tells you what kind of document it is and which parts matter.
  3. Ask your question. Send prompt 2 with your question at the very bottom. Claude pulls out quotes first and answers only from them.
  4. Double-check. Send prompt 3: Claude checks its own answer against the text and flags anything that isn't backed up.

Prompt 1: Overview

<document>
___ (paste the full text)
</document>

Read the whole document. Then give me:
1. In one sentence: what kind of document is this and who are the parties?
2. The 5 most important passages, each as a word-for-word quote with section or page number.
3. Everything with a date, deadline, amount or obligation as a table: What – Who – By when – Quote.
4. What's missing or vaguely worded.
Don't make anything up. If something isn't in the text, write "Not in the document".

Right after pasting – so you know what to ask.

Prompt 2: Question with quotes

Answer my question using only the document above. Work like this:
1. First find every passage relevant to the question. Write them word for word and numbered inside <quotes> tags.
2. Then answer the question using only those quotes. Put the number after each statement, e.g. [2].
3. If the document doesn't answer the question, say so clearly instead of guessing.
4. Finally: the answer in 3 simple sentences, no jargon.

My question: ___

For every specific question. The question always goes at the very bottom.

Prompt 3: Double-check

Check your last answer sentence by sentence against the document.
- Mark each statement ✅ (quoted directly), ⚠️ (only indirectly supported) or ❌ (not supported).
- Fix or delete every ⚠️ and ❌.
- Then give me the cleaned-up answer.

For anything important: money, deadlines, contracts.

Example

Paste a rental lease → prompt 1 shows deposit, notice period and utilities as a table → prompt 2: "Am I allowed to sublet?" → answer with a quote from the lease → prompt 3 checks everything is backed up.

Check before you send

  • Document at the top, question at the bottom
  • For long PDFs: ask for page numbers
  • Important decisions (legal, money): still have a human check

Paste the document first, then ask – Claude only answers from the text, with quotes.

Guide: Tailor your job application to the role with Claude

Match your resume and cover letter to a specific job posting – without making anything up.

How it works

  1. Prepare your data. Copy the full job posting. Paste your resume as text and remove your address, phone number and date of birth first.
  2. Get the match. Send prompt 1: Claude builds a table with every requirement, your proof and ✅/⚠️/❌.
  3. Close the gaps. Send prompt 2 and answer the questions honestly. Often you have the experience – it's just not in your resume.
  4. Build the cover letter. Prompt 3: Claude writes from your proof points. You fill in everything marked [ADD].
  5. Recruiter check. Prompt 4: Claude reads everything like a busy recruiter and shows you the weakest sentences.

Prompt 1: Match

Help me tailor my application to a specific job.

<job_posting>
___ (paste the full posting)
</job_posting>

<resume>
___ (paste your resume as text – without address, phone number, date of birth)
</resume>

How to work:
1. Pull every requirement from the posting and split them into "Must have" and "Nice to have".
2. Make a table: Requirement – Proof from my resume (word for word) – Rating ✅ met / ⚠️ partly / ❌ missing.
3. List the 5 most important keywords from the posting that aren't in my resume yet.
4. Give me the 3 most important changes to my resume, each as a before/after sentence.

Important: don't invent experience, numbers or degrees. If you're missing info for a requirement, ask me instead of guessing.

First thing for every new job.

Prompt 2: Close the gaps

Go through every ⚠️ and ❌ in your table.
1. Ask me exactly one question per point about whether I have experience with it – jobs, internships, studies, volunteering or side projects all count.
2. Ask the questions as a numbered list and wait for my answers.
3. Then turn my answers into new resume bullet points: action verb first, concrete result, only facts I actually mentioned.
4. Tell me where in the resume each bullet belongs.

Right after the match, if there are ⚠️ or ❌.

Prompt 3: Cover letter from proof

Now write a cover letter for this role, max 250 words.
- Opening: why exactly this role. Use only what's in the posting and mark with [ADD] where I should add something personal.
- Body: the 3 must-have requirements I can prove best, each with a concrete example from my resume.
- Tone: ___ (e.g. friendly and direct). No clichés like "I am writing to apply" or "I am very excited".
- Closing: one confident sentence about the interview.
Don't invent anything that isn't in my resume or my answers.

Once the resume fits.

Prompt 4: Recruiter check

Now read my resume and cover letter like a recruiter who has 30 seconds per application.
1. What sticks after 30 seconds? (3 bullet points)
2. Which must-have requirement from the posting is still not clearly proven?
3. Name the 2 weakest sentences and give me a better version of each.
4. Finally give me the revised cover letter.

Before you hit send.

Example

Online marketing role: posting + resume in → prompt 1 shows SEO ✅, budget ownership ⚠️, Google Ads ❌ → prompt 2 asks: ran ad campaigns during an internship → new bullet point → prompt 3 writes the cover letter from that proof → prompt 4 replaces the 2 weakest sentences.

Check before you send

  • Every must-have requirement has proof in the resume
  • The posting's keywords show up
  • No sentence that isn't true – Claude only rewords
  • Personal data removed; read the cover letter out loud: does it sound like you?

Paste posting and resume – Claude shows which requirements you prove and where the gaps are.
